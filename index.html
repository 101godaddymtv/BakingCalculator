Model assumptions

Bulk starts: when the flour is mixed into the starter-water mixture.

Baseline: 3.5 hours at 80°F for a 50% rise target.

Temperature kinetics: Arrhenius scaling is calibrated so the fermentation rate changes by a factor of 2.5 for a 10°C temperature increase at the 80°F reference point.

Rise scaling: Target time scales proportionally with desired rise relative to the 50% baseline.

Thermal lag: When room temperature is cooler than the dough temperature immediately after mixing, the calculator adds 3 minutes per °F of difference, capped at 90 minutes. This is a calculator safety assumption, not a universal sourdough law.

Starter feeding: The calculator assumes the seed starter begins at 100% hydration, then adds your feed flour and feed water to calculate the resulting starter hydration. That derived hydration is used to split the starter added to the dough into flour and water.

True dough hydration: (added water + starter water) ÷ (added flour + starter flour) × 100. The hydration calculation therefore includes all flour and water contained in the starter.

Fermentation model: Recipe hydration and starter composition are displayed for dough analysis, but they do not currently change the Arrhenius-based fermentation-time estimate.

Show mathematical model

Reference temperature: T₀ = 80°F = 299.817 K.

Q10 calibration: Eₐ/R = ln(2.5) ÷ [1/T₀ − 1/(T₀ + 10 K)].

Rate ratio: k(T)/k(T₀) = exp[(Eₐ/R) × (1/T₀ − 1/T)].

Kinetic time: 3.5 h × (desired rise ÷ 50) ÷ rate ratio.

Final estimate: kinetic time + thermal-lag safety offset.

Seed starter at 100% hydration: seed flour = seed starter ÷ 2; seed water = seed starter ÷ 2.

Resulting starter hydration: (seed water + feed water) ÷ (seed flour + feed flour) × 100.

Starter flour in dough: starter weight ÷ (1 + resulting starter hydration/100).

Starter water: starter weight − starter flour.

True hydration: (added water + starter water) ÷ (added flour + starter flour) × 100.

This calculator provides a fermentation estimate, not a guarantee. Measure dough temperature immediately after mixing for the best estimate. Dough strength, flour type, starter activity, inoculation method, salt, mixing, handling, and other variables can materially change actual fermentation time. Use dough expansion, structure, aeration, and feel as the final guide.
